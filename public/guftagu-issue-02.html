The cover story

The question that arrives at 2am

There's a version of this question that visits almost everyone eventually. Not loudly. Usually while brushing your teeth, or staring at a ceiling fan at 2am, or sitting in a meeting nodding at something you don't quite believe. Who am I, really? Not the job title. Not the role of daughter, partner, employee, the "responsible one." The version underneath all of that — if there even is one.

We tend to think of identity as something we discover once, like finding a birthmark we'd never noticed. But identity behaves less like a fact and more like a practice. It's built, session by session, out of the roles we're handed, the approval we chase, and — slowly, if we're paying attention — the choices we finally make on our own terms.

Most people don't wake up one day fully split from who they were raised to be. It happens in smaller moments: agreeing to a plan you didn't want, performing enthusiasm you didn't feel, realising you can describe yourself perfectly to a stranger but go blank when someone asks what you actually enjoy. Self-awareness is what turns the blankness into information instead of shame.

Identity confusion isn't a malfunction. It's usually the sound of a self that's outgrowing its old container.

This issue is about that outgrowing — the gap between the self we perform and the self we suspect is underneath, and what it actually takes to close it without pretending the question was ever simple.

The psychology behind it

What the research actually says

Erik Erikson framed adolescence around a central task he called "identity vs. role confusion" — but later researchers, particularly James Marcia, showed the process rarely finishes on schedule. Marcia described four identity statuses based on two things: whether a person has explored different values and roles, and whether they've committed to any of them. Most people cycle through these statuses more than once across a lifetime — after a breakup, a career change, a move, a loss, becoming a parent.

Diffusion

No real exploration, no commitment — often mistaken for laziness, but usually reflects overwhelm rather than apathy.

Foreclosure

Commitment without exploration — values adopted wholesale from parents or culture, never actually tested against one's own experience.

Moratorium

Active exploration, no commitment yet — the uncomfortable but necessary middle where most real growth happens.

Achievement

Exploration followed by a self-chosen commitment — not permanent, and often revisited later in life.

Self-awareness research adds another layer, distinguishing between internal self-awareness — how clearly you see your own values and patterns — and external self-awareness — how accurately you read others' perception of you. Organisational psychologist Tasha Eurich's research found these two capacities don't automatically move together: people who are highly attuned to how others see them are sometimes the least connected to what they actually want, because so much bandwidth goes toward managing the external picture.

One caution: identity status isn't a label to diagnose yourself with permanently. The useful question isn't "which category am I," but "where, right now, am I performing a commitment I never actually chose?"

In real life

The question with no rehearsed answer

A fictional scenario, built from patterns we see often.

Rhea is 27 and has a fully rehearsed answer for almost anything anyone could ask her — her plans, her opinions on other people's plans, what she thinks her parents want, what her manager expects by Friday. She is, by every external measure, extremely put-together.

At dinner, a colleague asks, half-joking, "Okay but what do you actually like doing — just for you, no one watching?" Rhea opens her mouth. Nothing arrives. She laughs it off, changes the subject, orders dessert. But the question follows her home.

That night she doesn't have a breakdown. She just sits with the discomfort of realising she has a complete answer for who she is to everyone else, and almost nothing for who she is when no one's grading the performance.

What's actually happening

Rhea likely sits closer to foreclosure than she'd guess — competent, decisive, but running on commitments she inherited rather than chose. The blank space isn't a deficit; it's simply unexplored territory, because exploration was never modelled or made safe for her.

What would change it

Not a dramatic reinvention. Just permission to answer badly at first — to say "I don't know yet" about something small, and let that be enough for one evening.

Myth vs fact

Correcting common misconceptions

You should "find yourself" once, and know exactly who you are forever after.

Identity evolves across life stages. Revisiting the question at 35 or 55 is growth, not a relapse into confusion.

Feeling unsure of who you are means something is wrong with you.

Identity confusion is a common, often necessary phase — especially after major transitions.

Self-awareness means constantly analysing every thought and feeling.

Real self-awareness is calmer than that — noticing patterns over time, not narrating every moment.

If your identity changes, your past self was "fake."

Every earlier version of you was genuine for the context it existed in. Growth adds to a self; it doesn't erase one.

Ask Saarzya

Reader questions

How do I know if I actually like something, or I've just always been told I should?— Reader

Notice how you feel after doing it when nobody else knows. Genuine preference tends to hold up in private; borrowed preference often needs an audience to feel worthwhile.

I feel like a different person around my family than with my friends. Is that a problem?— Reader

Not necessarily — context shifts everyone slightly. It's worth exploring only if one version is costing you something real, like honesty or peace.

Five honest minutes

Do it when you're calm, and don't edit your answers to sound better.

  1. When completely alone, I actually enjoy…Not what impresses anyone. Just what you'd do with the door closed.
  2. A belief I hold that isn't really mineSomething inherited rather than chosen — trace whose voice it first belonged to.
  3. People would describe me as ___, but I secretly feel more like ___Notice the gap without rushing to close it.
  4. One small thing I could do this week, just for the "me" underneath the rolesSmall is the point. This isn't a life overhaul.

The psychology corner

The performed self vs. the private self

Psychologists sometimes describe a gap between our "public self" — the version we manage for others — and our "private self," the one we experience alone. A little gap is normal and even useful; a wide, permanent one is exhausting, because maintaining a performance takes energy that never gets to rest.

Closing the gap doesn't mean abandoning every role you play. It means occasionally checking whether the role still fits, or whether you're simply afraid of what happens if you stop playing it.
